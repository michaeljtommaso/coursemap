# Product

## Register

product

## Users

Cornell undergrads doing two jobs in equal measure: visualizing their 4-year course sequence and prerequisite chains to understand what comes next, and discovering which major best fits the courses they already love. They arrive in the messy middle of planning, overwhelmed by options and conflicting requirements, trying to turn institutional complexity into a path that feels like theirs.

## Product Purpose

A course-first degree planning tool. Students pick courses they find interesting, see prerequisite chains and semester sequences laid out as a navigable roadmap, and surface which Cornell major best matches what they've already chosen. The tool inverts the default university framing: instead of "pick a major, then fit courses into it," students start with genuine interest and discover where it leads. Success means a student finishes a session with a concrete plan and the confidence that it reflects what they actually want.

## Brand Personality

Precise, calm, capable. The tool does a hard job without making noise about it. It is not cold or austere, and it is not flashy or gamified. It earns trust through clarity.

## Anti-references

- Generic SaaS dashboards: hero-metric templates, gradient card grids, big numbers with small labels, decorative color gradients. Design that signals "startup" rather than "tool."
- Bare-bones developer tools: terminal-only aesthetics, zero warmth, interfaces that feel like they were never intended for a non-technical audience. Too cold for a student planning their academic future.

## Design Principles

1. **Complexity, made navigable.** The prerequisite graph, credit requirements, and major options are genuinely hard. Every UI decision should reduce perceived cognitive load, not add to it.
2. **The plan is the vision.** A degree plan is not a checklist. The interface should reflect that the plan represents real ambition and real choices. It should feel worth caring about.
3. **Tool-native precision.** Surfaces, interaction states, and information density follow the Figma/Vercel register: neutral backgrounds that recede, strong active and hover states that communicate affordance, no decoration that doesn't earn its place.
4. **Course-first, not major-first.** The product philosophy runs through the UI: students discover major fit through courses, not the other way around. Layout and information hierarchy should reflect this direction of travel.
5. **Quiet confidence.** The tone is calm. The tool does not need to announce itself. Animations exist to orient, not to impress.

## Accessibility & Inclusion

No specific requirements defined yet. Build with reasonable defaults (semantic HTML, adequate contrast, keyboard operability) and revisit formally before a wider release.
